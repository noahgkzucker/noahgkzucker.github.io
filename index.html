I am an Assistant Professor of Politics at Princeton University. I study international and comparative political economy, with a focus on climate change and the politics of finance and labor.

I was previously on the faculty of the London School of Economics and a postdoctoral fellow at the Niehaus Center for Globalization and Governance. I hold a PhD in political science from Columbia University.

Publications

  1. Breadwinner Backlash: The Gendered Effects of Industrial Decline. American Journal of Political Science, forthcoming, with Richard Clark and Roza Khoban.

    APSA Award for Best Paper on Women, Gender, and Politics

    replication (opens in a new tab)

    Low-skilled men have long been vulnerable to globalization, decarbonization, and other forms of economic disruption. When shocks are realized, women often increase their paid labor to compensate for men’s loss of income. We study how layoffs of low-skilled men and concurrent shifts in workforce composition towards women affect voting. We theorize that this labor market rebalancing produces “nostalgic” coalitions of men and women that seek a return to male-breadwinner family models. Such attitudes fuel right-wing movements that pledge to protect conservative gender roles. Evidence from local labor markets and voting in the United States over the last two decades, as well as a longitudinal study tracking individual Americans over several decades, support this theory. These findings contribute a new gender-based account of the globalization backlash.

  2. Identity, Industry, and Perceptions of Climate Futures. Journal of Politics, 2025.

    doi (opens in a new tab) replication (opens in a new tab)

    Climate change and decarbonization will impose costs on a range of industries, but in ways that are difficult to predict. I argue that the racial makeup of an industry serves as a heuristic by which communities evaluate its viability in contexts of deep uncertainty. In surveys of diverse samples of the US public, I randomize the racial compositions of hypothetical industry workforces. I find that individuals are more pessimistic about industries that draw workers from marginalized racial groups, expecting those industries to be denied government support as climate-related stressors manifest. Individuals are more confident in industries tied to privileged groups, believing politicians will more readily come to their aid when called upon. These findings illustrate how racial divisions shape mass climate politics and suggest that group hierarchies serve as a touchstone for evaluating economic risk in uncertain settings.

  3. Global Governance under Populism: The Challenge of Information Suppression. World Politics, 2024, with Allison Carnegie and Richard Clark.

    Devex (opens in a new tab), World Politics: The Storied Teller (opens in a new tab)

    doi (opens in a new tab) replication (opens in a new tab)

    Populists’ ideological opposition to global governance is well recognized, yet whether and how these actors systematically undermine international organizations remain unclear. The authors argue that a key means by which populists warp global governance is by distorting scientific information, which is necessary for global responses to many public health and environmental issues. Populists are motivated to withhold or misreport scientific information due to their anti-elite, pro-state sovereignty views. Using new data on the source and quality of information provided to international organizations (IOs), the authors find that populist leaders are significantly less likely to provide scientific information to IOs than are other types of leaders. When they do offer such data, they are less accurate than the information that other sources supply. The authors’ findings suggest that populism may stymie international institutions’ ability to govern in areas of pressing international concern.

  4. Climate Cascades: IOs and the Prioritization of Climate Action. American Journal of Political Science, 2024, with Richard Clark.

    The Washington Post (opens in a new tab), Nature Climate Change (opens in a new tab), Carbon Brief (opens in a new tab), LSE Research for the World (opens in a new tab), Cornell Chronicle (opens in a new tab)

    doi (opens in a new tab) replication (opens in a new tab)

    International organizations (IOs) are rapidly reorienting around climate change, despite powerful principal states having divergent preferences on the issue. When and why do IOs prioritize climate change? We argue that they do so as a result of an endogenous process of staff learning and rotation. IO staff surveil and implement programs in target states. When working in climate-vulnerable countries, they come to see climate change as an issue warranting aggressive action. As these staff are rotated and promoted, interest in climate diffuses outwards and upwards through the institution. To test this theory, we introduce original data tracking the International Monetary Fund’s attention to climate change and the career paths of key staff. We complement this with interviews of International Monetary Fund personnel. We find support for our theory.

  5. Gender after Genocide: How Violence Shapes Long-Term Political Representation. World Politics, 2023, with Nikhar Gaikwad and Erin Lin.

    APSA McGillivray Award for Best Paper in Political Economy

    APSA Sage Prize for Best Paper in Comparative Politics

    APSA Award for Best Paper in Democracy and Autocracy

    doi (opens in a new tab) replication (opens in a new tab)

    What are the legacies of violence on women’s political representation? This article examines the long-term effects of a watershed conflict of the twentieth century: the Khmer Rouge genocide, during which 50–70 percent of Cambodia’s working-age men were killed. Using original data on mass killings and economic and political conditions in Cambodian communes, the authors find that genocide exposure is positively associated with women’s economic advancement and current-day indicators of women’s representation in local-level elected office. The authors conduct in-depth, ethnographic interviews with genocide survivors to explore the mechanisms by which violence spurred women into elected office. A crucial finding emerges: In areas that suffered the genocide’s worst killings, widows obtained economic autonomy, providing a template for the economic advancement of women in traditional households with surviving men. The shift in norms regarding the sexual division of labor and its transmission through intracommunal and intergenerational pathways allowed women to adopt larger public roles over time in communities more exposed to genocide violence.

  6. Group Ties amid Industrial Change: Historical Evidence from the Fossil Fuel Industry. World Politics, 2022.

    IPUMS USA Award for Best Published Research

    doi (opens in a new tab) replication (opens in a new tab)

    Coethnics often work in the same industries. How does this ethnic clustering affect individuals’ political loyalties amid industrial growth and decline? Focusing on migrant groups, the author contends that ethnic groups’ distribution across industries alters the political allegiances of their members. When a group is concentrated in a growing industry, economic optimism and resources flow between coethnics, bolstering migrants’ confidence in their economic security and dissuading investments in local political incorporation. When a group is concentrated in a declining industry, these gains dissipate, leading migrants to integrate into out-groups with greater access to political rents. Analyses of immigrants near US coal mines in the early twentieth century support this theory. The article shows how ethnic groups’ distribution across industries shapes the evolution of group cleavages and illuminates how decarbonizing transitions away from fossil fuels may reshape identity conflicts.

  7. Free Trade and Forms of Democratization. Journal of Politics, 2021.

    doi (opens in a new tab) replication (opens in a new tab)

    How do democratic transitions affect trade policy? A well-known finding is that transitions in developing countries produce lower tariff rates. But prior work implicitly assumes that all forms of democratic transitions are alike. There is less reason to expect trade liberalization to follow transitions led by political elites, which constitute roughly half of all democratic transitions in recent decades. Analysis of industry-level tariff rates indicates that elite-led transitions are not consistently followed by trade liberalization, while transitions initiated by the general public are associated with tariff rate reductions.

Working Papers

  1. Greener Pastures? A Labor Market Theory of Climate Governance. Revise and resubmit, Journal of Politics, with Simran Singh and Calvin Thrall.

    Firm interest in climate change is rapidly mounting. We argue that a consequent public-private competition for climate expertise undermines public climate governance. Governments seek climate experts to develop and implement climate policies. Firms seek similar experts to affect policy choice, skirt enforcement, and measure climate risks to commercial interests. As firm interest in climate grows, the government’s ability to attract experts declines, eroding its capacity for climate governance. To support this argument, we measure private sector demand for experts with unique data on U.S. job postings, paired with administrative data on government bureaucrats and metrics of the quality of climate governance. Our findings offer a novel labor-based account of global climate politics, contributing to the study of labor markets in political science, the sources of bureaucratic expertise, and the net effects of corporate social responsibility.

  2. Bureaucrats and the Choice of Institutional Priorities. Under review, with Richard Clark.

    How do international organizations decide which issues to prioritize? Existing accounts emphasize how political principals determine institutional priorities. We shift focus to individual bureaucrats, theorizing that interactions between staff shape their choice of how to allocate resources across governance tasks. When bureaucrats interact with colleagues attentive to a given issue, social pressures and career concerns lead them to reallocate effort towards the governance of that issue, even when principals send mixed signals on its value. We test this argument in the case of climate change at the International Monetary Fund. With original data on professional networks within the IMF, we show that ties to climate-attuned colleagues increase the effort bureaucrats subsequently invest in climate relative to other policy areas, particularly when those colleagues are senior to them. These findings offer a social account of bureaucratic decision-making and show how intra-staff linkages can support institutional adaptability to emergent policy challenges.

  3. Does Humanization Spur Climate Action? Evidence from a Canvassing Campaign in India. Working paper, with Nikhar Gaikwad.

    pre-registration (opens in a new tab)

    Mass mobilization on climate change remains limited, even in climate-vulnerable democracies. Activists argue that emphasizing the human costs of climate change should rally voters. We theorize the mechanisms by which humanization may translate into mobilization. We then execute a field experiment in India, designed alongside a climate NGO, to test these claims. Door-to-door canvassers distributed videotaped accounts from citizens contending with intense climate disruption. These humanized testimonials built solidarity with vulnerable communities and increased the perceived immediacy of climate change. But compared to placebo videos and non-humanized climate information, they had no enduring effect on voters' willingness to make costly pro-climate policy tradeoffs, nor their likelihood of taking real-world political action on climate. Tests show that resistance to testimonials from ethnic outgroups contributed to the null effects. These findings highlight the limits of prominent advocacy strategies and challenges of mobilizing action in diverse democracies.

  4. Global Governance By Invitation Only. Working paper, with Calvin Thrall.

    Business leaders vary in their political activities. Some leave the corporate world for influential roles in governments and international organizations. Others insert themselves into contentious political debates. More still, including heads of large multinationals, rarely speak on politics. Why are some business leaders more politically engaged than others? This paper theorizes that elite clubs explain political mobilization by corporate leaders. Political and business elites often interact in exclusive, invitation-only fora. We argue that the social ties forged upon entry into such clubs both socialize business leaders into valuing political activity more and generate social capital that facilitates subsequent entry into political office. We find support for this argument using unique data on the full attendance lists of Bilderberg Meetings, which annually convene transnational groups of business and political leaders, and high-frequency metrics of individual business leaders' political involvement. The results illustrate the importance of elite clubs, such as those convened in Davos and Aspen, to global governance, and show how policymakers, beyond being targets of corporate influence, can themselves shape the preferences and behaviors of business leaders.

  5. Revolving Doors and Bureaucratic Behavior in Global Finance. Working paper, with Richard Clark.

    How do career incentives shape the behavior of international bureaucrats? We argue that bureaucrats in global economic governance balance mission-driven preferences with forward-looking career concerns. Bureaucrats who lack elite educational credentials face stronger incentives to signal their quality within their institution. Other bureaucrats can generally lean on such credentials as a sign of competence, except when they want to make themselves more attractive to outside employers. To test this argument, we develop original data on the career trajectories of staff at the International Monetary Fund to show that bureaucrats with more outside employment options generally extract fewer and narrower conditions than others. However, elite-educated bureaucrats who eventually depart for private sector employment impose stricter conditionalities. The findings reveal how unequal opportunity structures shape bureaucratic behavior and governance outcomes in international finance.
